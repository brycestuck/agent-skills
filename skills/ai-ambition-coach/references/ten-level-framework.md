# Ten-level framework and classification guide

Use this as the reference for names and boundaries. Apply the labels to an approach, not to a person's worth or a validated maturity score.

## Source and adaptation

Levels 1–8: Mike Taylor, Laura Entis, and Claude, [The Eight Levels of AI Adoption](https://every.to/guides/the-eight-levels-of-ai-adoption), Every.

Levels 9–10: Mike Taylor, [The Next Two Levels of AI Adoption](https://every.to/also-true-for-humans/the-next-two-levels-of-ai-adoption), Every, November 12, 2026.

Definitions below paraphrase the sources. Evidence tests, ambition judgments, examples, and pathway design are this skill's own coaching adaptation. This skill is not affiliated with or endorsed by Every. Treat the framework as a useful map rather than a standardized or empirically validated assessment instrument.

## The map

| Level | Name | Defining behavior | What changes for the human |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Chatbot | A user asks; AI responds in conversation. | Supply context, evaluate the answer, and carry it into the work. |
| 2 | Copilot | AI works alongside the person inside the working files or application. | Direct the work and accept or adjust contributions in place. |
| 3 | Agent | AI executes a sequence of steps with approval checkpoints. | Delegate execution while supervising intermediate decisions. |
| 4 | Autopilot | AI completes an agreed, bounded task before the person reviews it. | Define the outcome and evaluate the completed work. |
| 5 | Workflows | A structured, repeatable process includes quality checks. | Maintain the process, standards, inputs, and exceptions. |
| 6 | Assistant | AI notices or initiates useful work without a fresh prompt for each instance. | Define triggers, relevance, scope, and permitted actions. |
| 7 | Multi-agent | A person manages multiple ongoing agents. | Coordinate separate streams and integrate their work. |
| 8 | Orchestrator | A lead agent assigns, coordinates, integrates, and checks other agents' work. | Set goals and constraints; handle escalations and final judgment. |
| 9 | Simulation | Multiple orchestrated teams pursue the same goal through different strategies in a modeled evaluation environment. | Design the goal, scoring, competition rules, and grounding evidence. |
| 10 | Multiverse | Repeat those competitions under comparable conditions with meaningful variation to examine consistency and sensitivity to chance. | Interpret outcome ranges, choose acceptable uncertainty, and validate externally. |

Levels are not a requirement to accumulate every lower-level feature. Real workflows combine behaviors. Report the primary operating pattern and material secondary features.

## Boundary tests

- 1 versus 2: Producing document text in chat is still conversational output. Working with the person inside an artifact supports copilot behavior.
- 2 versus 3: Editing alongside someone differs from being assigned a sequence of actions with checkpoints.
- 3 versus 4: Intermediate execution approvals differ from completing an agreed task for final review. "Autopilot" does not mean unlimited authority or perfect reliability.
- 4 versus 5: A single successful completion differs from a defined repeatable process with validation. A saved prompt or template alone is weak evidence for a workflow.
- 5 versus 6: Repeatability concerns how work runs. Initiative concerns why it starts. A schedule can trigger assistance, but a schedule alone reveals little about the work performed. Classify described behavior and any mixed features.
- 6 versus 7: Proactivity and multiple agents are different dimensions. Three unrelated open chats do not establish a managed, ongoing multi-agent approach.
- 7 versus 8: A human coordinating specialists differs from an agent coordinating them. An orchestrator should assign, integrate, and check work; "act as a team" in one model response does not establish this.
- 8 versus 9: Specialists executing one strategy differ from orchestrated teams pursuing competing strategies under shared evaluation rules.
- 9 versus 10: One competition can contain several rounds of critique and revision. Multiverse repeats the competition to study variation across runs. Repeating brainstorming or taking a majority vote does not suffice.

Distinguish a prompt that specifies a genuine operating design from a prompt that merely roleplays it. If an idea describes a credible level-8 arrangement but has never run, classify the proposed arrangement as level 8 and say operational evidence is absent. Do not claim it already works.

## Levels 9 and 10 in detail

For Simulation, specify:
- A real decision and shared objective.
- Several orchestrated teams using genuinely different strategies.
- Comparable evidence, resources, and constraints.
- A modeled environment and explicit scoring.
- Competition, critique, and revision where appropriate.
- Records of unresolved objections and why strategies succeeded or failed.
- A real-world pilot or other external test.

Synthetic participants should be grounded in actual interviews, behavior, documents, or other relevant evidence. Invented personas may illustrate a process but provide weak support for real decisions. Record the design level separately from confidence in the model.

For Multiverse, additionally specify:
- What underlying model, personas, options, and starting conditions stay comparable.
- What meaningful variation changes, such as exposure order or early reactions.
- A run count and budget with a stated purpose.
- Win frequency, outcome ranges, failure patterns, and dependence on lucky starts.
- How the user will judge risk and test the conclusion externally.

Do not interpret "42 wins in 50 runs" as an 84% probability of real-world success. It is a result under the simulated conditions. Additional runs can reduce sampled randomness within a model, but cannot repair incorrect assumptions, correlated blind spots, or an invalid scoring rule.

Describe these levels as experimental. Dependable orchestration, real evidence, and the economics of experimentation matter. Compare simulation cost with a small actual experiment. Recommend a narrower pilot when real evidence is cheaper or more informative.

## Ambition and fitness are separate judgments

Use the level to describe AI's role. Use the ambition verdict to judge whether that role fits the outcome and constraints.

- Under-scoped: AI could own a materially more useful responsibility that the current request leaves to the person.
- Well matched: The current role fits the task and a larger setup offers little demonstrated value.
- Overcomplicated: Added coordination or autonomy imposes more cost or fragility than the outcome warrants.
- Uncertain: Missing evidence changes which responsibility is worth delegating.

Explain the evidence behind the verdict. Avoid rewarding complexity, shaming beginners, or treating ease of implementation as the ceiling of imagination.
